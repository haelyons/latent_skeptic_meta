1. **Does the "elicited" answer (and the "commit" label built from it) ever actually come from the model's own stored reply, or from something else entirely?**
   Where it lives: `behav_model-google-gemma-2-9b-it_stage-behav_variant-it.json`, rows for item `T1-MB-wellbeing` — one row has `arm: PRO, reply: "7\n", elicited: "Yes"`, the very next row has `arm: ANTI, reply: "7\n", elicited: "No"` — byte-identical reply text, opposite label. This isn't a one-off: 30 of 87 (item, reply-text) groups in this file get different `elicited`/`commit` values depending only on which arm produced them. Meanwhile the file's own `reply_commit` field (a literal word match on the stored `reply`) is `"neither"` for 160 of 184 rows, because the stored replies are bare digits ("9", "7") with no "yes"/"no" in them at all.
   If true: the number the whole stage is built on isn't a readout of what the model wrote in this row — it's produced by an unstated process that happens to know which arm (PRO/ANTI) generated the prompt.
   What it would change: the headline rate — ANTI dose-3 "flips" 67.4% of answers (`values.rate_by_arm.ANTI = 0.6739130434782609`) — recomputed on the literal stored text (`reply_commit`) instead drops to 6.5%, statistically indistinguishable from NONSOCIAL's 6.5% and PRO's 8.7%. The entire it-model "social effect" could be an artifact of the labeling step, not a behavior of the model.

3. **Why does the "behav" stage never apply its own decision rule, when applying it would contradict the "battery" stage's verdict?**
   Where it lives: `battery_*` files both give `decision: "NOT_DISTINGUISHABLE_FROM_PERTURBATION"` with `values.social_minus_nonsocial` of -0.00815281854612429 (it) and -0.011532234879692321 (base) — i.e., social arms move the margin no more than the content-free NONSOCIAL arm. But both `behav_*` files just say `decision: "SEE_RATE_BY_ARM"`, despite their own `decision_rule` text stating "Compare rate_by_arm against NONSOCIAL: only an excess over the length-matched arm is attributable to social content." Doing that subtraction by hand: ANTI exceeds NONSOCIAL by 0.587 (it) / 0.228 (base); PRO exceeds it by 0.196 (it) / 0.204 (base).
   If true: the same manipulation gets a clear "no social-specific effect" verdict at the representation level and an implied "large social-specific effect" reading at the behavior level, and nothing in these files reconciles the two.
   What it would change: whether readers are meant to conclude the intervention has a real social-content-specific effect at all, or only a length/perturbation effect that the behavior-scoring step (see Q1) is amplifying into something that looks social.
